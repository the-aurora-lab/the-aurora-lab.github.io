What it does

Interviews and focus groups

  • Transcribes on your computer with Whisper large-v3-turbo: on the GPU of an Apple Silicon Mac (about 2 minutes for an hour of audio on an M3 Max) or on any CPU.
  • Finds who spoke when with pyannote's segmentation model and WeSpeaker voice prints, then gives every word its speaker. Give the number of speakers, or let it estimate them; name them (Interviewer, Participant 7) and merge or fix them afterwards.
  • Makes a pseudonymised copy: people, places, organisations, full dates, emails and phone numbers become [Person 1], [Place 2]…, the same tag for the same person across every interview of the project. Your own list of names is matched even when Whisper misspells them. The key and the log stay apart from the shareable files; a list of suspects shows what to check by eye; forget-key deletes the link when the study no longer needs it.
  • Writes the files your software imports, timestamped and with speakers, and a review page where any time plays that moment of the recording and unsure words are underlined.

Lectures and seminars

  • Lays the transcript out under a still of each slide, in the order shown. Camera cuts make no still, builds are followed to the fullest version, a slide shown twice keeps one still, and the slide is cropped out of a speaker-beside-slides layout.
  • Study notes, when you want them: your coding agent (Claude Code with the included skill) reads the stills and the transcript and writes notes with every equation in LaTeX; notes check confirms every slide is covered and every equation compiles, and notes tex --pdf makes a document.

What stays on your computer

local-transcripts setup downloads the models once, each from its publisher with a pinned checksum or revision: Whisper from huggingface.co, the speaker models and the name finder from github.com. After that no command uses the network: no account, no licence key, no telemetry. We ran an interview and a lecture with the network blocked by the operating system (macOS sandbox-exec, deny network): both finished.

The one step that sends anything anywhere is optional and yours: lecture notes are written by your own coding agent, which reads the lecture's transcript and stills and so sends them to the AI service it runs on. Interview transcripts are never part of that; the included skill tells the agent not to open interview files and not to ask you for names.

It closes with "complex numbers make everything cool" (Euler's formula, the fundamental theorem of algebra, function theory).

$$\mathbb{C} = \{a + bi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R}\}, \quad i^2 = -1$$

$$z = \rho\left(\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi\right) = \rho\, e^{i\varphi}$$

$$z_1 z_2 = \rho_1 \rho_2\, e^{i(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)}$$

$$(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + i(ad + cb)$$

$$(a + bi)^* = a - bi, \qquad |z| = \sqrt{z^* z}$$

$$e^{ix} = \cos x + i \sin x$$

The speaker added two facts: an algebraic equation has exactly as many complex solutions as its degree (counted correctly), and a complex function that is differentiable once is differentiable infinitely often.

How well it works

Measured once, on version 1.0.0, against recordings the tool was never tuned on, gathered and labelled by an independent agent that never ran it: 10 interviews (4.9 hours; CDC and NOAA podcasts, simulated clinical interviews, a meeting, an oral history, a telephone oral history; 2 to 6 speakers) and 6 lectures with their slide PDFs (FOSDEM, PAKE’26, a quantum gravity seminar), all openly licensed. Each interview ran twice, with the number of speakers given and estimated, and with no names list, so only the name finder and the patterns worked. For the lectures, one Claude Code session per talk (Sonnet 5.5) wrote the notes, and a judge agent compared every still with the PDF and every equation with the builder’s LaTeX. Intervals are 95%.

  • The speaker count. For two people, giving it is best (0.975–0.997 against 0.73–0.75 estimated). Where an announcer or a short clip adds voices that say a sentence or two, forcing that count split the main speakers (0.53–0.69) while the estimate found them; version 1.0.1 added --max-speakers for that case.
  • What leaked: names Whisper wrote in lower case (“nicole”; matched since 1.0.1), long agency names worded differently, and names misheard as ordinary words (“Fran” as “friend”). Names on the published keep list (CDC, FDA, NOAA, US states) were kept as designed and not counted.
  • Over-replacement: 199 of 511 replacements hit something that is not a name, mostly technical acronyms and Latin genus names (one acronym, 50 times); some were names the builder had not labelled. Clear them in key.csv.
  • The failure: a loud, noisy 48-minute telephone recording gave 489 words; the speech filter heard almost no speech. Since 1.0.1 the whole recording is read in that case (6,915 words on this file). Fixes after the run are not re-measured; the next held-out set will.

Limits

  • Transcripts are drafts: names, numbers and technical terms need a human check, which the review page is made for. Overlapping speech goes to one speaker.
  • Speaker labels are best with the number of speakers given; similar voices, phone lines and meetings of many people give more mistakes.
  • Pseudonymisation is a strong first pass, not a guarantee: read the shareable copy before sharing. Indirect identifiers (a job title, a rare event) are not detected, and a name Whisper misheard badly may be missed.
  • Slide stills need slides on screen; a filmed whiteboard gives none, and a scrolled document gives one still per scroll stop.
  • Name finders for English, German, Spanish and French; other languages use a multilingual one.
  • Works on files you have; it does not download from YouTube or the web.
  • Tested on macOS (Apple Silicon) with Python 3.10–3.14 and ffmpeg 8; Windows and Linux are untested.
